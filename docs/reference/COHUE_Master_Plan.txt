1. Vision, problem, narrative, and positioning
1.1 The problem
A crowd can contain the skills, perspectives, connections, and resources to accomplish a meaningful objective, yet these complementary capabilities remain invisible. Language barriers, fragmented networks, unknown availability, and lack of coordination prevent collaboration. Existing assistants usually generate advice; directories surface profiles; project tools organize teams that already exist. COHUE aims to bridge the missing stage: forming viable collaborations from unconnected participants.
1.2 Product thesis
Human ambition, collectively realized. Tell COHUE what you want to accomplish. Its agents discover who and what you need, coordinate across language barriers, and bring the right people together to make it happen.
1.3 Brand story and positioning
COHUE comes from the French word for a bustling gathering. The idea is that within a crowd are undiscovered possibilities. COHUE transforms those possibilities into purposeful, mutually agreed action. Brand narrative: “From strangers to something extraordinary.”
Three product verbs: DISCOVER (surface hidden capability and resources), COORDINATE (form feasible groups and request consent), MAKE (activate a mission with assigned next steps).
1.4 Differentiation and claims
Do not position COHUE as a social network, generic matchmaking app, AI text wrapper, or chatbot.
The proposed differentiator is multi-person mission formation with constraint-based optimization, consent, multilingual coordination, event-driven autonomous tool execution, and dynamic replanning.
A proposal is not a commitment; an external search result is not an accepted collaborator; a mission activates only when required participants approve.
Do not claim COHUE invents agent orchestration, matching, or multilingual translation; several established products operate in adjacent categories.
Success should be demonstrated by verifiable app states: requirements covered, approvals received, tasks assigned, and documented recovery after a decline.

3. End-to-end product journeys
3.1 First-run entry and onboarding
Visitor arrives at cohue.tech; immediately sees a compelling but readable live-network introduction and clear “Start a Mission” / “Join a Mission” actions.
Guest session is created with secure session state; traditional account creation is not mandatory.
Participant supplies display name, preferred language, offered skills/capabilities, needs, availability, interests, and explicit participation/visibility choices.
User may record voice or type, correct the transcript and structured profile, and submit.
Validated participant node persists; a real-time update can introduce it into the living graph.
3.2 Mission Genesis / composer
Mission creator speaks or types a goal. Voice follows record → transcribe → understand → review → act.
Gemini extracts objective, required skills, constraints, outcomes, and unknowns into schema-validated data.
Creator corrects required skills, timing, group-size bounds, and expected output before launch.
Mission state changes from draft to planning, and an actual orchestration run begins.
UI presents requirements as connected mission elements rather than a standalone prose response.
3.3 Discovery to activation
Director decomposes the mission into capability/resource requirements.
Scout queries internal participant profiles; where needed, optional SerpApi searches public events, organizations, and resources.
Strategist computes and ranks feasible groups using structured capability coverage, availability, group-size constraints, and mutual benefit.
Coordinator sends persisted invitations to individuals with role explanations in their preferred languages.
Participants accept, decline, or counterpropose; only responses made by authenticated invitees count.
On decline, the state transitions to replanning; keep valid accepted commitments, recompute missing coverage, and send replacement proposals without assuming consent.
After all mission-critical approvals, COHUE creates a Mission Room with approved roles, responsibilities, tasks, source links, and next steps.
3.4 Multilingual participant example
Mission created in English. Spanish-speaking participant receives a translated written/spoken invitation and responds with adjusted availability. French-speaking participant declines. Coordinator interprets response, records the decline, and initiates real replanning. If a suitable internal replacement is unavailable, Scout presents attributed public opportunities rather than pretending they are confirmed participants. The approved team receives a shared mission summary in each selected language.

5. Design excellence and responsive engineering
5.1 Design authority
The frontend designer owns the design system, final font pairings, color palette, component visual treatments, and cohesive brand styling. The PRD owns required screens, interactions, states, responsive behavior, accessibility, performance, and motion semantics. Do not force a preset palette or generic Inter-based SaaS template.
5.2 Visual quality bar
Distinctive, premium identity; no generic dashboard or monotonous cards.
One visually continuous experience from spoken intent to active mission, not unrelated screens.
Clear difference between proposed, pending, accepted, declined, rejected-by-constraints, activated, and failed.
Every decorative animation must not masquerade as an actual agent action.
Readable information hierarchy, accessible contrast, keyboard focus, touch targets, captions and non-audio alternatives.
All motion must have reduced-motion behavior; animations degrade gracefully under reduced performance.
Aim for fluid ~60 FPS interactions on capable devices; prioritize responsiveness over particle volume.

7. Agent orchestration and safety boundaries
7.1 Architecture
One modular monolith exposing application routes, with typed internal agents and a shared persisted mission state. The agents are roles/capabilities, not separate distributed processes or four perpetual LLM conversations. A state machine controls transitions, validates tool outputs, enforces timeouts, and writes audit events.

7.2 State machine
DRAFT → PLANNING → DISCOVERING → PROPOSED → AWAITING_APPROVAL → ACTIVATED. Branches include DECLINED → REPLANNING → REPROPOSED, plus FAILED, CANCELLED, EXPIRED and PAUSED_FOR_HUMAN. State changes require validated server-side commands and atomically committed records.
7.3 Event model
Persist event ID, mission ID, run ID, agent, event type, sequence number, correlation ID, input/output references, timestamp, status, duration, and structured metadata. Suggested event types: MISSION_CREATED, EXTRACTION_STARTED, REQUIREMENT_CREATED, SCOUT_SEARCH_STARTED, CANDIDATES_FOUND, PROPOSAL_CREATED, INVITATION_SENT, INVITATION_ACCEPTED, INVITATION_DECLINED, REPLAN_STARTED, REPLAN_COMPLETED, MISSION_ACTIVATED, TASK_CREATED, PROVIDER_FAILED. Replay reads these events, not handcrafted animation timestamps.
7.4 Tool / privacy controls
Gemini plans and requests tools but cannot fabricate database writes or approve invitations.
All tool arguments are Zod-validated; limit recursion, tool counts, runtime, credits and retries.
SerpApi public results retain URL, title, retrieval timestamp and source; unverified results never become confirmed partners.
Participant responses require scoped authenticated tokens and idempotency.
Avoid exposing private profile details in public graphs; use permissions and opt-in visibility.
Permission changes, consent, and activation decisions are server-enforced and auditable.
No automatic outbound spam, impersonation, or unsolicited contact.
8. Graph optimization and matching
8.1 Core mechanism
AI may normalize free-form skill and mission descriptions into structured categories. Actual group feasibility is computed deterministically. The engine produces direct matches, complementary groups, and bounded reciprocal cycles subject to skill coverage, availability, team size, relevant preferences, and explicit participation permissions.
Optimization objective: choose feasible group G to maximize U(G), where U combines required-skill coverage, availability compatibility, relevant interests, group stability and reciprocal benefit. Hard constraints override scores, and consent is required before activation rather than being estimated by a model.
8.2 Repair behavior
Invitation decline or scheduling change creates an event and invalidates affected proposal memberships.
Recheck all hard constraints; preserve accepted commitments that still remain valid.
Find candidate replacements or revised configurations, with deterministic tie-breaking and maximum search bounds.
If no valid solution is found, report missing requirements and request creator intervention; never claim success without validation.
In the UI, remove invalid links, surface uncovered requirements and animate verified replacement edges.
8.3 Tests for engine
Exact capability coverage; cross-taxonomy normalization; contradictory availability; minimum/maximum team size.
Duplicate skill holders; no feasible group; deterministic tie resolution; participant opt-out; no consent bypass.
Changing a constraint invalidates appropriate groups; accepted valid memberships are preserved where possible.
Semantic extraction failure uses schema retry/manual editing and does not corrupt graph state.
9. Multilingual audio and voice
Input pipeline: microphone with explicit user activation → ElevenLabs Scribe transcription → editable transcript → Gemini structured mission/profile extraction → creator confirmation. Output pipeline: canonical approved invitation → Gemini localized text → ElevenLabs TTS → playback and transcript. TTS is not assumed to perform translation on its own.
Initially test English, Spanish, and French thoroughly. Additional languages can share the pipeline when validated. Make text-only workflows function identically for users who cannot or do not wish to record audio. Avoid storing raw audio longer than necessary, and provide visible capture controls.

9.2 Resilience
If microphone permission fails, fall back to typed input. If transcription fails, enable manual transcript entry. If TTS is unavailable, show translated text. If translation confidence is uncertain, allow editing/review rather than silently changing commitments. All participation and consent actions are confirmed by direct user interaction.
10. External discovery and provider budgets
SerpApi supports external discovery of events, organizations, public resources, workshops and relevant opportunities. Scout runs tightly bounded queries and records source links. Its results are ranked against current mission gaps but are never interpreted as registered users or guaranteed invitations.
User has approximately 1,000 SerpApi credits, subject to actual balance, plan and endpoint accounting. Suggested initial planning envelope: ~3 searches per mission for ~25 demonstration missions (75 searches), reserve ~300 credits for testing and iteration, and keep the remaining budget for retries/experimentation. This is a planning estimate, not a measured usage count.
Provider protections: server-only keys, request caps, per-mission quota and deduplication; caching and cooldown for repeated searches; timeout and fallback statuses; do not allow runaway autonomous loops.

13. Deployment, secrets, operations
Primary host: Vercel for UI and short API functions. Database: Tiger Data PostgreSQL. Orchestration: checkpointed short-running jobs and persisted event-driven state. Add a Vultr worker only if long-running and retry-sensitive jobs demand a persistent process and the setup can be validated without destabilizing the app. Realtime: SSE with polling fallback. A local agent process is not assumed to survive serverless termination.

COHUE is a multilingual intent-to-outcome collaboration engine. Its central capability is to transform a person’s spoken or written ambition into a mission, discover necessary people and public opportunities, calculate viable collaborative groups, secure each participant’s consent, recover gracefully from constraints and declines, and activate an actionable mission. The visual product should make this intelligence observable through a premium, mobile-first, event-driven, fluid interface.
